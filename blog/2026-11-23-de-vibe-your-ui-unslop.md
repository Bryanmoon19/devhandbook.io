# Your AI Wrote Ugly Code — How to De-Vibe Your UI with unslop

You know the look. You ask your coding agent for "a landing page," and out comes the same page everyone else's agent produced: a purple-to-blue gradient hero, text with a subtle color shift, buttons with radius so large they're almost circles, a font that says "Inter or nothing," and enough glassmorphism to make 2021 blush. It's technically fine. It's also the most generic thing on the internet.

The community has a name for it: **slop**. And as of a few weeks ago, there's a tool to fix it. It's called **unslop**, and it hit roughly **3.7k GitHub stars in its first week** — because every AI-assisted developer recognized their own codebase in its README within the first three seconds.

There's no plain-English explainer for it yet, which is a gap worth closing, because it sits directly in this site's niche: the *practical* consequences of building with AI coding agents. You've read here about the [token cost](/blog/2026-11-22-ai-cost-of-javascript-frameworks) and the [hidden expenses](/blog/2026-08-09-hidden-costs-ai-coding-agents) of agent-driven work. This is the other cost nobody invoices you for: **everything you ship with an agent looks the same, and that sameness is a liability.**

---

## What "slop" actually means

Let's define the term precisely, because it's thrown around loosely. In the AI-coding community, **slop** is the specific visual and structural signature of code generated by a frontier model with default settings and no strong design direction. It's not "bad code" in the buggy sense — it's *indistinguishable* code. The telltale markers:

- **Purple/indigo/blue gradients** on heroes, buttons, and text. Models gravitate to this palette because it's the modal average of "modern SaaS landing page" in their training data.
- **Gradient text** — the classic `bg-clip-text text-transparent` treatment that looks premium the first time and copy-pasted the thousandth.
- **Oversized border radius** — `rounded-2xl` and `rounded-3xl` everywhere, cards that look like pills.
- **Generic emoji or icon accents** — the sparkles ✨, the rocket 🚀, the "✨" feature bullets.
- **Inter (or system-ui) as the only typeface**, with no typographic hierarchy beyond weight.
- **"Modern" filler copy** — "Effortless," "Seamless," "Supercharge your workflow," "Unlock your potential."
- **Glassmorphism and soft shadows** — `backdrop-blur` panels, `shadow-lg` floating cards, everything slightly translucent.

None of these are wrong in isolation. A single purple gradient is fine. The problem is the *convergence*: when a thousand projects all use the same purple gradient, the same Inter, the same pill buttons, and the same "Supercharge your workflow," they stop being distinct products and become one indistinguishable blob. Your landing page doesn't say "this is what we built" anymore — it says "this is what the model built by default."

That's the cost. For a real product, visual indistinguishability means lower trust, lower conversion, and a brand you can't defend. For a portfolio or a side project, it means nobody remembers you. Slop is the design equivalent of shipping with the default favicon.

## What unslop does

unslop is a command-line tool that targets exactly this. The pitch is one line: **it detects AI-generated design clichés in your code and rewrites them into more distinctive, intentional choices.**

The mechanics, at a high level:

1. **It scans your codebase.** Point it at a directory and it parses your frontend — CSS, Tailwind classes, component markup, color definitions, typography — and identifies the slop markers listed above. It's looking for the *patterns*, not judging taste: a gradient is flagged, a `rounded-2xl` is flagged, the word "seamless" is flagged.

2. **It classifies severity.** Some findings are hard slop (a purple-blue hero gradient is the canonical case), some are softer (Inter is a defensible choice; Inter-plus-nothing-else is not). The output is a report, not a lecture.

3. **It proposes replacements.** This is the important part. unslop doesn't just say "your design is generic" — it generates concrete alternatives: a different color ramp, a reduced radius scale, a typographic pairing, copy that isn't "supercharge." Where it can, it applies the changes directly with a `--fix` flag.

4. **It's model-aware but model-agnostic.** The rewrites can be plain deterministic transformations (replacing a gradient with a flat color, tightening radii), or — where deeper judgment is needed — it hands context to your existing coding agent (Claude Code, Cursor, a local model) to do the actual redesign. It's a *detector and orchestrator* more than a designer.

The result is that you get from "generic AI look" to "something with a point of view" without having to be a designer yourself — which is precisely the gap, because the people reaching for an AI agent in the first place are usually the ones least equipped to notice when the agent phones it in.

## Why it took off (3.7k stars in a week)

A tool getting ~3.7k stars in a week doesn't happen because it's useful in theory. It happens because it names a pain everyone already feels but nobody had put a tool on. unslop's growth is downstream of a few real dynamics:

**Everyone recognized themselves.** The single strongest driver. Show a frontend developer unslop's README — which is full of before/after screenshots of the exact gradients, radii, and copy they've been shipping — and the reaction is involuntary recognition. "That's my landing page." That kind of "it's me" moment is rocket fuel for word of mouth.

**The vibe-coding backlash has been building all year.** Through 2026, the honeymoon of "just vibe it out" curdled. The [costs of AI coding](/blog/2026-08-04-ai-coding-cost-management-2026) are now understood, and so is the aesthetic cost: a flood of projects that *work* but all look the same. unslop is one of the first tools to treat "your code is generic" as a bug you can lint and fix, rather than a vague vibe you're supposed to intuit.

**It plugs into existing workflows.** It's not a new framework or a new way of building — it's a linter you run on what you already have. Zero adoption friction. That's the difference between a tool that sparks and a tool that sticks.

**The "de-vibe" framing is sticky.** "De-vibe your UI" is a great meme. It implies the agent's default *is* a vibe — casual, unconsidered, interchangeable — and that craftsmanship is the thing you add back on top. It reframes the human's role in AI-assisted development not as the person who writes the code, but as the person who supplies the *judgment* the model can't.

That last point matters more than it looks. unslop isn't really a design tool. It's an argument about the division of labor, packaged as a CLI.

## How to run it on your own slop

The tool is young, so treat the exact invocation as likely to shift. But the shape of the workflow is stable and worth knowing, because it's the pattern you'll use regardless of which tool you end up with.

**Step 1 — scan.** Point it at your frontend directory and get a report of what it found:

```bash
# illustrative — check the repo's README for the current flags
unslop scan ./src
```

You'll get a list like: `hero uses blue→purple gradient`, `12 components exceed rounded-xl`, `button copy contains "seamless"`, `only one font family detected`. This is the moment most people have their "oh no, it's me" reaction.

**Step 2 — review the report honestly.** Not every flag is a problem. If your product *is* a developer tool and your brand *is* "purple and modern," a purple gradient might be correct. unslop is a signal, not a verdict. The point is to make the *choice* deliberate instead of defaulted.

**Step 3 — apply the safe fixes.** The deterministic transformations — tightening radius, swapping a gradient for a flat color, de-duplicating font weights — are low-risk and reversible:

```bash
unslop fix ./src --safe
```

**Step 4 — hand the judgment calls to your agent.** For the changes that need taste — "pick a color ramp that doesn't look like every other SaaS," "give me a type pairing with character" — unslop can pass the specific finding and a strong prompt to your [coding agent](/blog/2026-08-13-ai-coding-agent-cost-tracking-tools-compared). This is where the "de-vibe" actually happens: the agent, given *explicit* direction to avoid defaults, produces much less slop than it does with a blank "make it look good."

The meta-lesson is that **you can't un-slop by just telling the agent "don't make it generic."** Vague anti-directions don't work, because the model doesn't know which of its defaults you mean. You have to *detect* the specific clichés first — which is exactly what unslop does — and then direct against them specifically.

## The deeper point: slop is a prompt-engineering problem

Here's the thing unslop gets right that most of the "fix ugly AI output" conversation misses. Slop isn't a model-quality problem. It's a **prompting and direction problem.**

Frontier models are perfectly capable of producing distinctive, tasteful design — give one a strong design brief, a specific palette, a type pairing, and a "no gradients, no Inter" constraint, and it'll comply. The reason everything looks the same is that most people *don't give it that*. They say "build me a landing page," and the model, correctly, produces the thing the *most* people will find acceptable: the modal landing page. That's not the model failing. That's the model doing exactly what an average over its training data would do.

unslop's real value is that it externalizes the design direction that was missing. It's a *linter for the absence of taste in the prompt.* It finds where you let the model default, and gives you a concrete way to override it.

This reframes the human role in the AI-coding workflow in a way I think is going to stick. The model supplies speed and volume. You supply the things the model can't average its way to: a point of view, a constraint, a reason this page should look like *you* and not like *everyone*. The faster you get good at supplying that, the less slop you ship — with or without a tool.

## Where this fits in the bigger AI-coding picture

I've spent 2026 writing about the *economic* and *structural* consequences of AI coding agents — the [cost](/blog/2026-08-04-ai-coding-cost-management-2026), the [hidden expenses](/blog/2026-08-09-hidden-costs-ai-coding-agents), the [token bill of your framework choice](/blog/2026-11-22-ai-cost-of-javascript-frameworks). unslop sits in that same story but on a different axis: it's the *aesthetic* consequence of the same defaulting-to-the-average behavior that drives the other costs.

They're actually the same phenomenon. A coding agent that defaults to generic design, to the most common framework pattern, to the modal answer in its training data — that's the *same* agent that burns tokens re-deriving a hidden re-render model and re-generates boilerplate because it wasn't given direction. In every case, the failure mode is identical: **undirected generation converges to the average.** The fix in every case is the same too: supply explicit, specific direction, and detect the defaults you didn't mean to accept.

unslop is just the first tool to make that concrete for the *visual* layer. I expect a whole category of "de-slop" linters to follow — copy, accessibility patterns, API design, naming conventions — because the underlying insight generalizes cleanly.

## Should you run it?

Yes, with one caveat. If you've built anything with an AI agent this year, run the scan. The worst case is you learn your UI is already distinctive and you feel validated. The likely case is you find a handful of defaulted choices you didn't know you'd accepted, fix them in an afternoon, and ship something that reads as *yours* instead of *the model's*.

The caveat: don't outsource the judgment entirely. unslop is a detector and an orchestrator, not a designer. Run it, read the report, make the calls yourself, and use your agent to execute. The moment you hand the *taste* back to the model, you're back to slop — just slop that's been told to avoid a slightly different list of defaults.

The tools are getting good at making the generic cheap. That means the scarce, valuable thing is now the *specific*. And specificity — a real palette, a real voice, a real opinion — is still, for now, a human job. unslop won't do it for you. But it'll show you exactly where you forgot to do it yourself.
