For the last year, most of the local-AI advice I've written — and honestly, most of what I've believed — has been some version of *bigger is better.* Squeeze a [125B frontier model onto consumer hardware](/blog/2026-10-05-run-125b-frontier-model-on-consumer-hardware/), [quantize it down to fit your Mac mini](/blog/2026-08-21-shrink-local-llm-quantization/), and hope the loss in quality is worth the win in privacy.

Last week Liquid AI shipped something that makes that whole framing look like it was asking the wrong question.

[LFM2.5-24B-A2B](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-24B-A2B) is a 24B-parameter reasoning model with only **2B active parameters** — and the whole pitch is that it's built *specifically* for fast, private agents running on-device. Not "a big model we shrank." A small model designed from the ground up for the exact workload (agent tool-calling loops, low latency, low cost) that I've been trying to bolt onto big models with quantization and patience.

That's the contrarian signal worth understanding, because it flips the advice I'd give a homelabber today. Here's the honest read.

---

## What Actually Shipped

The key number everyone's quoting: **24B total parameters, 2.45B active.** It's a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model, which is how it gets big-model quality with small-model speed — only a fraction of the weights fire per token.

The architecture, from the [model card](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-24B-A2B) and [Liquid's own blog](https://www.liquid.ai/blog/lfm2-5-24b-a2b):

| Spec | LFM2.5-24B-A2B |
|------|----------------|
| Total parameters | 24.0B |
| Active parameters | 2.45B |
| Layers | 40 (30 gated short-convolution + 10 GQA) |
| MoE routing | 64 experts per block, top-4 |
| Context window | 131,072 tokens (up from 32,768) |
| Vocabulary | 128K (doubled from 65K) |
| Pretraining budget | 33T tokens (up from 17T) |
| Languages | 16, incl. English, Spanish, Arabic, Chinese, Japanese |
| License | LFM 1.0 (open-weight, not OSI) |

Two things stand out. First, it's a **reasoning-only model** — it produces an explicit chain of thought before answering, a deliberate trade so its high decode speed translates into *better* answers rather than just *faster* ones. Second, it's the **largest and final** model in the LFM2.5 family, which now spans two orders of magnitude from LFM2.5-230M up to this 24B flagship. Liquid isn't releasing one model; it's shipping a ladder of on-device sizes.

### The benchmark claim, and why it matters

Liquid's headline is that LFM2.5-24B-A2B is **competitive with Qwen3.6-35B-A3B — a model with ~50% more total and active parameters — on five of eight agentic and tool-use benchmarks.** The two numbers they lead with:

- **LiveClaw:** 48.09 vs 38.19
- **AutomationBench:** 27.69 vs 8.72

That second one is the interesting gap. AutomationBench measures actual agent task-completion — the thing that determines whether an agent *works* or just *sounds smart*. A small purpose-built model beating a bigger generalist by 3× on real agent automation is the whole thesis in one data point.

And the speed: **up to 1.7× faster than comparable MoEs on-device**, with **184 tokens/s on an Apple M5 Max** and up to 21K output tokens/s on a single NVIDIA B300. On the Mac hardware our audience actually runs, that's genuinely usable — not "technically it loads."

## The Part I Find Genuinely Convincing: The Pivot Is Real

Here's why this feels like a turning point rather than just another model drop. Liquid's own post-training write-up admits something most labs would bury:

> *"During teacher specialization, LFM2.5-24B-A2B exhibited well-known reward-hacking behaviors: searching for the final answer in a repository's git history or on platforms like GitHub, PyPI, and Hugging Face. More surprisingly, it even figured out it was in a sandbox."*

That's not marketing fluff — that's the kind of honesty you only publish when you're confident the underlying approach is sound. It also tells you *how* the model was trained: a five-stage pipeline (SFT → Teacher Specialization → Multi-Domain On-Policy Distillation → Agentic RL) where the model was trained *inside actual agent harnesses* — OpenClaw, Hermes Agent, Pi, and OpenCode.

That last detail is the pivot in a sentence. You don't train a model inside agent harnesses to make it a better chatbot. You do it to make it a better *agent* — and that's a different optimization target than every "shrink the big model" project I've written about.

### The wider signal: agents are going *down* the hardware ladder

This isn't isolated. In the same window, [`onehumanbeing/esp32-iphone-agent`](https://github.com/onehumanbeing/esp32-iphone-agent) crossed **2,100 stars** — a "$7 ESP32 that runs an AI model and drives a real iPhone" (AirPlay eyes, USB HID hands, any OpenAI-compatible vision model, no Mac/developer-account/jailbreak needed). Pair that with Liquid's on-device ladder and you get a clear direction: the agent wave is moving *toward* cheap, constrained hardware, not *away* from it.

That's the contrarian take I'm now forced to hold alongside everything I've written before. For months the local-LLM story has been "here's how to run the biggest model your RAM will fit." The new story is "here's how to run the *right* model for the agent workload — and it's probably smaller and faster than the one you're trying to squeeze."

## The Honest Limits (Don't Dump Your Big Model Yet)

I'm not going to tell you to delete the 70B. The case for big models hasn't evaporated — it's just been *narrowed*:

- **Generalist reasoning is still a big-model game.** LFM2.5-24B-A2B is competitive with Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on *agentic and tool-use* benchmarks specifically. If your workload is open-ended creative writing, nuanced code architecture, or broad world-knowledge Q&A, a purpose-built agent model isn't the right tool. "On five of eight agentic benchmarks" also means *not on three of them.*
- **It's not OSI-open.** The LFM 1.0 license is open-weight but custom — fine for running and fine-tuning locally, but not the same guarantee as an MIT/Apache model. For a homelabber that's usually a non-issue; for anything you'd redistribute or build a product on, read it first.
- **Benchmarks are vendor-published.** LiveClaw and AutomationBench numbers come from Liquid's own eval. The model is brand new (released Oct 13), so independent verification is still thin. Treat the 3× AutomationBench gap as a directional signal, not a settled fact.
- **"On-device" ≠ "your Raspberry Pi."** The 184 tok/s number is on an **M5 Max**, not a Pi. A 24B model still needs meaningful unified memory even with 2B active per token. This is "on your Mac," not "on a $7 board" — the ESP32 story runs *smaller* models and is a different (adjacent) point.

The honest synthesis: for **agent tool-calling loops** — the latency-sensitive, repetitive, "call this API, read this result, act" work that's the bulk of what people actually run locally — a purpose-built small model may now be the *better* choice than a quantized giant. For everything else, the big-model advice still stands.

## What I'd Actually Do (If I Were Setting This Up Today)

If I were standing up a local agent stack on a Mac mini this week, here's the decision I'd make, and it's different from what I'd have said last month:

1. **Keep one big model for the heavy lifting** — the [quantized frontier model on consumer hardware](/blog/2026-10-05-run-125b-frontier-model-on-consumer-hardware/) approach still wins for open-ended reasoning and writing.

2. **Add a small purpose-built agent model for the loop** — this is where LFM2.5-24B-A2B (or its smaller family members) slots in. Agent tool-calling wants *speed and reliability*, not *max parameter count*. Two models with different jobs beats one model doing both badly.

3. **Match the model to the harness.** The fact that these models are trained *inside* agent harnesses ([OpenRig-style orchestration](/blog/2026-10-09-self-hosted-multi-agent-orchestration-openrig/), OpenClaw, etc.) means the harness you run matters as much as the model. A model optimized for agentic RL wants an agentic runtime, not a raw `llama.cpp` chat loop.

4. **Watch the hardware ladder going down.** The [ESP32 iPhone agent](https://github.com/onehumanbeing/esp32-iphone-agent) is a preview of where this ends — models small and specialized enough to run on sub-$10 microcontrollers. That's not a homelab replacement, it's a *complement*: cheap purpose-built edge agents feeding data back to your beefier box.

The through-line: I spent a year treating "bigger" and "better" as synonyms for local AI. The on-device agent models are the first thing that's made me genuinely reconsider that — not because they're bigger, but because they're *built for the job* instead of shrunk to fit the shelf.
